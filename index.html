Transportation Performance Targets

Tracking transportation metrics for the Atlanta region

Federal transportation performance measures are metrics used to evaluate the performance of transportation systems at the federal level. These measures are used to assess the effectiveness and efficiency of transportation investments, as well as to identify areas for improvement. The Atlanta Regional Commission (ARC) uses performance measures to track trends in transportation system performance. The trends observed in these measures provide important insights that can help guide transportation planning and investment decisions in the region.

Safety (PM1), asset management (PM2), and system performance (PM3) are three measures mandated by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA). Two measures, transit safety and transit asset management, are mandated by the Federal Transit Administration (FTA).

FHWA · PM1

Safety

The Atlanta Regional Commission (ARC) has a comprehensive safety program that aims to reduce the number of traffic fatalities and serious injuries in the 20-county Atlanta region. The program is designed to address safety issues on all types of transportation infrastructure, including roads, bridges, transit systems, and pedestrian and bicycle facilities.

In January of each year the Georgia Department of Transportation (GDOT) establishes safety targets, and ARC has 180 days to agree to those targets. While ARC follows the targets that GDOT sets, as an MPO it also establishes its own aspirational targets.

Roughly 600 people die and more than 2,800 people are seriously injured in traffic crashes in the ARC region every year. In the nine years from 2013 to 2022, the number of deaths increased from 455 to 767 and the number of serious injuries increased from 1,902 to 4,006. The vision for the Atlanta region is safe, accessible, and convenient travel for all road users. As a moderate, achievable goal, ARC will adopt a 5% reduction target each year for all safety performance measures.

It is important to break up intractable problems into smaller and more adaptable responses. Establishing emphasis areas is one opportunity to break up larger safety issues into manageable components. The Regional Safety Strategy (RSS) is an action plan with a data-driven approach to help ARC achieve its safety goals.

According to the Regional Safety Strategy, the top contributors to deaths are intersections, roadway departures, and pedestrian and bicycle crashes. ARC selected these crash types as regional emphasis areas because they present the greatest opportunity to reduce death and serious injury, and they are the focus crash types of the Regional Safety Strategy.

FHWA · PM2

Asset Management

GDOT is required to establish 2- and 4-year targets for four pavement condition measures and two bridge condition measures. For interstates on the National Highway System (NHS), GDOT's goal is to keep more than 50% of the interstate in good condition and to keep poor-condition interstate under 5%. For bridges on the NHS, GDOT's 2- and 4-year goals are to keep more than 50% and 60% in good condition, and to keep poor-condition bridges under 10%.

By tracking the condition of bridges and routes across the state, GDOT can prioritize its maintenance and repair efforts so that resources go where they are most needed. This improves the safety and reliability of Georgia's transportation infrastructure and ensures it continues to meet the needs of its users.

The Asset Management performance measure helps ensure that the region's transportation infrastructure is well maintained, efficient, and effective at meeting the needs of its users. GDOT and local governments are responsible for managing transportation assets in Georgia, including roads and bridges. They regularly assess the condition of these assets to identify areas that require maintenance or repair, evaluating age, pavement condition, and other factors that affect the lifespan and usability of assets.

Every two years GDOT sets targets for asset management measures, and ARC follows those targets. GDOT had an opportunity to adjust the 4-year target in 2024.

FHWA established five metrics to assess pavement condition: International Roughness Index (IRI), cracking percent, rutting, faulting, and Present Serviceability Rating (PSR).

Since 2018, GDOT has used 3D line laser imaging technology to acquire high-resolution pavement surface data to evaluate the condition of the state's roadways. The data collected include transverse profile, rutting, faulting, cracking, and IRI. To assess the condition of bridges, GDOT follows the National Bridge Inventory (NBI) guidelines for classifying a bridge in Good, Fair, or Poor condition.

FHWA · PM3

System Performance & CMAQ

Federally mandated system performance refers to travel time reliability, which is used to assess the consistency and predictability of travel times. Measures include Person-Miles Traveled on routes that are reliable, the Truck Travel Time Reliability (TTTR) Index, and Annual Hours of Peak Hour Excessive Delay (PHED).

System performance is an important factor in evaluating the overall effectiveness of a transportation system and can affect user satisfaction, safety, and economic productivity. It measures the variability in travel times and shows how often travelers experience unexpected delays or congestion. ARC adopts the State targets for system performance.

Person-Miles Traveled on routes that are reliable

Reliable Person-Miles Traveled on the interstate and non-interstate NHS quantifies the total distance traveled by people on roads that are deemed reliable: roads with consistent and predictable travel times and minimal delay, particularly during peak travel periods.

Truck Travel Time Reliability

According to the Federal Highway Administration, travel time reliability is the "consistency or dependability in travel times, as measured from day-to-day and/or across different times of the day." FHWA recommends several measures to calculate the TTTR Index, including 90th or 95th percentile travel times for specific routes or trips, the buffer index, and the planning time index. The 90th or 95th percentile travel time is the simplest, and indicates how bad delay will be on the heaviest travel days. More information on calculating the TTTR Index is available from FHWA.

Annual Hours of Peak Hour Excessive Delay

Annual Hours of Peak Hour Excessive Delay (PHED) per capita measures the time individuals spend in excessive delay during peak traffic hours, relative to population. Excessive delay is the additional time spent in traffic beyond what is considered typical, and can result from high traffic volume, inadequate infrastructure, congestion, crashes, weather, or other unforeseen events.

Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality Improvement

The Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality Improvement (CMAQ) program is a federal initiative that aims to improve air quality and reduce traffic congestion. It provides financial assistance for transportation projects and programs that reduce emissions and enhance mobility, helping to create healthier and more sustainable communities.

ARC's CMAQ target is the 2-year and 4-year cumulative estimated emission reductions for all CMAQ-funded projects for each applicable criteria pollutant: volatile organic compounds (VOC) and oxides of nitrogen (NOx), which are precursors to ground-level ozone (smog), for which the Atlanta area is designated nonattainment or maintenance (23 CFR 490.807).

FTA

Transit Asset Management

The Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century Act (MAP-21) directed the U.S. Department of Transportation to develop regulations for a system to oversee and manage public transportation assets. The primary objectives are to enhance safety, improve reliability and performance, and establish performance metrics.

Transit Asset Management (TAM) is a systematic, strategic approach to managing and maintaining the assets of a transit system, including vehicles, facilities, equipment, and other components essential to providing public transportation. The goal of TAM is to optimize the performance, safety, and longevity of these assets while prioritizing funding effectively.

The Atlanta-Region Transit Link Authority (ATL) was created in 2018 to support the transit systems operating in 13 counties of the Atlanta region. These transit partners are MARTA, Xpress, and group transit operators including Cherokee Area Transportation System (CATS), CobbLinc, Connect Douglas, Forsyth County, Gwinnett County Transit (GCT), Henry County Transit, and Paulding Transit.

FTA

Transit Safety

ARC defines targets by mode: fixed-route bus, commuter bus, demand-response, light rail, and heavy rail. Fatalities, injuries, safety events, and system reliability are used to quantify the transit safety performance measure.

Transit safety events are incidents that compromise the safety of public transportation, ranging from minor incidents to major accidents, and can involve rolling stock, infrastructure, personnel, and passengers.

System reliability refers to the ability of transit systems to operate safely without failures or malfunctions that could compromise passenger safety. It is measured as Mean Distance Between Failures (MDBF), in miles.

The Federal Transit Administration (FTA) has established requirements to ensure the safety of transit passengers, employees, and the public. Transit agencies must comply with these safety regulations, standards, and guidelines to receive federal funding. Transit agencies within the Atlanta region are the Metropolitan Atlanta Rapid Transit Authority (MARTA), CobbLinc, Gwinnett County Transit, Cherokee Area Transportation System, Henry County Transit, Connect Douglas, Xpress (operated by GRTA), and the Center for Pan Asian Community Services (CPACS).
